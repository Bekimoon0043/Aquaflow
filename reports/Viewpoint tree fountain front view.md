# Grow the canopy from crowns, backlit along one plane

A real fountain can produce the STAND HERE front view. The fluffy canopy has to come from the **naturally breaking crowns of plain turbulent jets**: not foam jets, not fog, not a crystal laminar nozzle. To get it, size each row's nozzle so the jet starts breaking up at about 55–70% of its height. Space and stagger the rows so the crowns overlap when seen from the eye point. Then light only the volume above one "canopy-floor sight plane", from behind and to the side, so the viewer sees forward-scattered green light and the columns below stay dark. No catalogue product combines a clear thin stem with a bushy lit head. Foam jets and ice towers are white from the nozzle up, and clear laminar jets have no crown at all ([Safe-Rain](https://saferain.com/en/fountain-equipment/fountain-nozzles/); [OASE Schaumsprudler datasheet](https://www.teichfolie.de/media/products/Produktdatenblatt_Schaumsprudler%2035%20-%2010%20E%20.pdf)). The design therefore rests on physics and lighting geometry, not on a product. Three findings change the current AquaFlow layout (`aquaflow/frontend/scripts/build-viewpoint-tree.mjs`: 82 jets in rows A–H, Y = 1.4–8.05 m, 9 × 9.5 m pool). First, its canopy spots sit on the viewer's side, which is the weak back-scatter direction. Second, its uniform 12 mm nozzles break up too high on the front rows and too low on the back rows. Third, its basin is far too small for the usual splash rule, because the 6.4 m jets sit 1.45 m from the back edge. The honest limits: nobody has published a measured crown diameter versus height for any fountain nozzle, outdoor fog will not hold a canopy shape, and wind breaks the alignment before it breaks the fountain. A one-night nozzle test has to calibrate the numbers below before anything is poured.

## Plain turbulent jets, not foam or fog, make a thin stem with a bushy head

The nozzle families split cleanly. Aerated **foam jets** (OASE Schaumsprudler 35-10 E to 75-20 T) are bright, wind-stable and level-independent, but white along the whole column. They also use a lot of water: **47–123 L/min for the 35-10 E and up to 1,039 L/min at 1.95 bar for 10 m from the 75-20 T** ([OASE datasheet](https://www.teichfolie.de/media/products/Produktdatenblatt_Schaumsprudler%2035%20-%2010%20E%20.pdf)). **Ice towers** are air-injector nozzles that make a tapered white tower. A DN25 unit runs at **8–12 m³/h for 1–5 m with a 0.2–0.3 m spread** ([Aquaswan DN25](https://www.poolfountainaccessories.com/sale-28783239-dn25-dancing-fountain-nozzles-water-fountain-spray-heads-brass-ice-tower.html); [Aquarays](https://aquarays.co.nz/product/ice-tower-fountain-nozzle/)). In an ice tower the column is the effect. **Crystalline jets** go the other way: they use flow straighteners to remove turbulence and air, so the jet is transparent and stable ([Safe-Rain](https://saferain.com/en/fountain-equipment/fountain-nozzles/)), and that suppresses the crown. **Dandelion or sphere heads on risers** give a fixed 0.6–1.8 m head, but the stem is then a pipe and the head sits only 1–2.3 m above it ([China Music Fountain](https://chinamusicfountain.com/portfolio/dandelion-fountain-nozzle/)). That suits a sculpture, not "faint water columns". The only family that matches the brief is a smooth-bore jet with no straightener: a thin, nearly clear column that blooms at the top.

The bloom is real physics. At the apex of a vertical jet the water slows to zero, so continuity forces the cross-section to grow as d(z) = d₀(1 − z/H)^-¼. That is only 1.19× wider at half height, but 3.2× wider at 99% of the height (my derivation). Drag and turbulence then throw the slowed water sideways into a crown, and air friction keeps real jets below the Torricelli height ([arXiv 0801.4160](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0801.4160)). Fountain jets of this size have Reynolds numbers near 10⁵, which puts them in the **turbulent surface break-up regime**. Trettel's re-fitted correlation for that regime, from Kusui's data at 5.4–12.7% exit turbulence, is **x_b/d₀ = 3.61·Tu₀^-0.275·We₀^0.334**. The intact length grows only weakly as exit turbulence falls and grows with the Weber number ([Trettel, engrXiv](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/740/version/1128/1657/1115)). Two caveats apply. These correlations come from horizontal and downward pipe jets, so applying them to a jet that decelerates to zero is an extrapolation. And the same paper's onset correlation predicts surface droplets shedding within about 0.1 m of the nozzle. Expect a faint sparkle along the columns, not glass.

That correlation gives a usable sizing rule, which AquaFlow's Weber-based real-world mode should reproduce. At Tu₀ ≈ 5%, a jet of height H breaks up at x_b ≈ 0.7H when **d ≈ 10 mm at 2 m, 12 mm at 3 m, 14 mm at 4 m, 15 mm at 5 m and 17–18 mm at 6.5 m** (my calculation). Because x_b ∝ H^0.33·d^1.33, the current uniform 12 mm nozzle breaks up at 0.87H on the 2.2 m front row, which leaves a small crown. On the 6.4 m back row it breaks up at 0.43H, so the upper half is spray. The back-row result matters less than it looks: those columns are seen through the front rows' crowns, so early break-up there adds texture inside the canopy. The working constraint is narrower. **Each jet must stay intact up to the point where its sight line enters the canopy's lower edge**, and its top 30–45% should be broken. Sizes of 10 mm for rows A–B, 12 mm for C–E, 14 mm for F–G and 16 mm for H give 0.53–0.68H across the tree (table below). Flow checks against data: the ideal-orifice flow × 1.15 gives 69 L/min for a 12 mm jet at 4 m, against OASE's **68 L/min at 0.48 bar** for the Comet 10-12 T ([OASE Comet 10-12 T](https://www.oase.com/en/water-technology/comet-10-12-t-int)).

How big will the crowns actually be? This is the largest unknown. No manufacturer specifies apex head diameter, and the "coverage" figures in Chinese listings most likely describe where the falling water lands. Bracketing from the ice-tower ranges and the spray-angle regression gives a planning range of **0.3–1.0 m per crown for 2–6.5 m jets**. The current preview's tufts are 0.79–1.13 m wide and 1.04 m tall on a 2.2 m jet, which sits at the optimistic end. A single test rig settles it: one 10, 12, 14 and 16 mm nozzle at 2.2, 4 and 6.4 m, photographed at night from 15–22 m. The trunk is the one place where white-throughout is wanted. Four small foam jets (35-10 E at about 80 L/min, 0.28 bar for 1 m) or fixed 1" ice towers (3–5 m³/h for 1–3 m) give a short, frothy, very bright column ([OASE datasheet](https://www.teichfolie.de/media/products/Produktdatenblatt_Schaumsprudler%2035%20-%2010%20E%20.pdf); [Aquarays fixed](https://aquarays.co.nz/product/ice-tower-fountain-nozzle-fixed/)).

## Crowns fuse only where they overlap in projection; visual acuity never blurs a gap

Distance does not merge the jets. Normal acuity is about **1 arcminute** ([University of Sydney](https://ophthalmology.sydney.edu.au/visualacuity_theory/index.html)), which is 4–6 mm at 14–20 m. A 0.3 m gap between lit heads at 17 m subtends about 60 times that limit. A 12–16 mm column at 15–22 m subtends 2–3.5 arcmin, so it stays a distinct line if any light falls on it. The canopy reads as one mass only when crown envelopes physically overlap along the line of sight. Within a row, that means **centre pitch ≤ 0.7–1.0 × crown width**. Across rows, N rows offset laterally by pitch/N project to an apparent pitch of pitch/N. That is the cheapest way to fill a canopy, and mist or haze behind it lowers the contrast of any remaining gaps. The current row A pitch of 0.464 m works out to 0.59 × the assumed 0.79 m tuft. That pitch still merges even if real crowns are only 0.5 m, provided adjacent rows are staggered by half a pitch. The tree's scalloped edge then comes for free, as the boundary crowns' own bumps, with a scallop period equal to the apparent crown width.

The build method is the one anamorphic artists use: lay out every element from the eye point. Felice Varini traces a projected image ([Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/large-scale-anamorphic-illusion-paintings-by-felice-varini/)). Bernard Pras builds while looking through a fixed camera, and his images are "comprehensible from a single, pre-defined point of view" ([De Galerie Den Haag](https://www.degaleriedenhaag.nl/en/artist/bernard-pras_en/)). The sources describe tolerance only in words: the work "can change dramatically when the viewer moves only a short distance" ([Perspective Research Centre](https://perspectiveresearchcentre.com/anamorphosis-aberrations/)). Geometry fills the gap. For a near element at D₁ and a far element at D₂ on the same sight line, a viewer offset Δ misaligns them by e = Δ(D₂ − D₁)/D₁, so **Δmax = e·D₁/(D₂ − D₁)** (my derivation). The current rows span D = 15.4–22.05 m. Allowing e = 0.25 m (a quarter-crown slip) gives about **±0.58 m** of viewer movement. A child's eye 0.5 m lower slips the back row 0.22 m against the front row. Compressing the rows into 4 m of depth raises the tolerance to about ±0.96 m, but weakens the side-view reveal of rows. On the same geometry, the STAND HERE disc should be about 1 m across (the current 0.6 m radius is right), with a 0.3–0.5 m step for children. In practice the larger error source is the water: wind and pressure drift move crowns by similar amounts, and taller back-row jets drift more than front ones.

## Light the canopy from behind and to the side, and cut along the sight plane

Lighting decides the effect as much as the nozzles. Water droplets scatter in the Mie regime, with "a very strong diffraction peak, a wide forward-scattering lobe" ([arXiv 1709.05418](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.05418)). A crown is therefore brightest when the light travels roughly toward the viewer. Rain Room found the same thing: the Barbican team "settled on a single light source at the back to visually carve out every drop" ([Ingenia](https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/inside-the-rain-room-no-umbrella-required/)). The current script places each 11° spot 1.2 m on the viewer's side of its jet, aimed back and up. That light hits the crown at about 113° from the viewer direction, in the back-scatter hemisphere. Its steep 66–78° elevation also runs the beam along about 2.6 m of column (my geometry). Moving the same fixture 2 m **behind** the jet, at a 58° beam axis, changes the scattering angle to about 63° and cuts the lit column length to about 1.3 m. Base lighting is the wrong tool for the canopy. A ring light "travels up the entire column" ([Paradise Landscape Lighting](https://www.paradiselandscapelighting.com/mastering-underwater-fountain-lighting-installation-guide/)), and laminar columns guide light end to end like fibre optics ([Firgelli](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/luminous-fountain)).

The strongest buildable rule here is a framing cut along a single plane (my derivation). Define the **canopy-floor sight plane** as the plane through the eye and the canopy's apparent lower edge. With the script's perspective (drawing plane 18.5 m from the eye), z(Y) = 1.6 + (v_b − 1.6)·(14 + Y)/18.5. For a canopy whose apparent bottom is v_b = 2.8 m, the plane sits at 2.6 m over row A and 3.03 m over row H. Everything above it reads as canopy from STAND HERE, and everything below it is the faint columns and trunk. The cut needs a blade-framed projector. The Martin Exterior Projection Pro Compact is IP66, gives **5,200 lm**, has a 15–45° motorised zoom and takes an optional four-blade framing module ([Creation Networks](https://creationnetworks.net/products/martin-exterior-projection-pro-compact-ip66-outdoor-image-projector-white)). Mount the lens on the plane's extension, so the plane is seen edge-on, and a straight lower blade then cuts exactly along it. With masts at X = ±5.5 m, Y ≈ 14 (about 6 m behind row H) and a lens height of about 3.4 m, the beam meets the canopy centre at about 33° from the viewer direction. The beam that passes the canopy then misses STAND HERE by about 10 m to the side, so the viewer sees the glow and never the lens. Two masts crossing from both sides light both faces of every crown, and the same light makes the side view's lit heads.

In-pool fill spots can add punch: OASE ProfiLux 1100 (**735 lm, 11°**) ([Water Garden UK](https://www.water-garden.co.uk/prod/profilux-1100-led-24v-flood-light-23-watts)) or Philips C-Splash 2 (**583 lm, 10°, IP68**) ([Philips](https://www.assets.lighting.philips.com/is/content/PhilipsLighting/comf51809-pss-en_ae)). Place each one behind its jet by b ≈ 0.6 × crown height. The lit band then has length b·[tan(α + θ/2) − tan(α − θ/2)], so narrow optics and a large b keep it short. RGB units are too weak for "bright green": the ProfiLux RGB gives about **114 lm of its 213 lm in green at 25°** ([Water Garden UK](https://www.water-garden.co.uk/prod/profilux-garden-led-rgb-spot-light)). The canopy needs dedicated high-output green, ideally 520–530 nm, where the eye's photopic sensitivity is high. The trunk is the exception and should be lit from its base. Aerated water "is an effective carrier of light" ([Crystal Fountains](https://crystalfountains.com/insights/water-effects-and-lighting-options-for-a-water-feature/)). One unconfirmed snippet says it needs about twice the light per vertical foot of a clear stream, with fixtures at least 2 ft from large aerated nozzles ([WaterShapes](https://watershapes.com/light-sensations/)). Contrast does the rest. Use a black or dark-dyed pool floor so underwater spill is not reflected onto the columns, a dark hedge or sky behind the canopy, and shielding for path lights in the view. A starting target, which no source gives, is columns at least 10× dimmer than the crowns. Obtrusive-light limits under CIE 150:2017 depend on the site's zone ([Austrian Standards](https://austrian-standards.at/en/shop/cie-150-2017~p2636130)).

## Fog cannot hold an elevated canopy outdoors; at best it garnishes a calm night

True fog is about 15–20 µm water droplets made at about 70 bar through 0.15–0.3 mm orifices ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/an-artist-creates-artificial-fog-in-san-francisco-24339206/)). It uses about **0.11 L/min per nozzle** ([Danfoss 180Z1949](https://store.danfoss.com/en/p/180Z1949)). It moves with the air. Fujiko Nakaya's fog "rolls down hills, hovers over water" ([NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/stories/ethereal-fog-sculptures-are-rolling-through-bostons-parks)). The Exploratorium fog bridge needs an anemometer so that only the upwind side fogs ([MeeFog](https://www.meefog.com/2023/01/20/exploratorium/)). Fog sinks and drifts, so it will not float as a canopy 4–6 m up unless it is contained, as in Disney's fog-filled water domes ([US7775457](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7775457)), or renewed by nozzles at the top of the volume. Its forward lobe is only about **2.5° wide** for 5 µm droplets ([miepython](https://miepython.readthedocs.io/en/latest/05_fog.html)), so it glows strongly only for a viewer looking almost into the light, which is the glare position. Two uses are worth building. The first is a low in-deck fog skirt around the trunk base, lit from below as in Koolfog's under-jet LEDs ([Koolfog](https://landscapearchitect.com/ladetails/landscape-product/fog-systems/koolfogkoolfog-10444/fog-in-deck-fountain)), which hides nozzle hardware and makes the trunk frothier. The second is an optional fog bar on the back-row steel at about 6 m, on calm nights only, which hazes the gaps between crowns. Fog would ruin the faint-columns zone if it drifted into it, so keep it out of the space between trunk and canopy. Fog needs its own high-pressure pump and a treated, softened supply. Bellagio feeds its fog from softened water ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountains_of_Bellagio)). It is also a breathable aerosol, so CDC fountain Legionella guidance and ASHRAE Guideline 12 apply ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/control-legionella/php/toolkit/decorative-fountain-module.html)). No source gives a wind speed at which a fog cloud loses its shape. My estimate is 2–3 m/s.

## Hydraulics are modest; splash margins and wind are the real constraints

Grouping follows the vendor rule of keeping equal-height jets on a common low-loss header ([Safe-Rain design tips](https://saferain.com/en/blog/tips-of-fountain-design-part-2/)). That fits this tree exactly, because each row is one height. Use **one VFD pump per row plus one for the trunk**, a balancing valve on every branch, and solenoids only where the show animates. With the per-row nozzle sizes below, flows run about 36 L/min per jet on row A and 155 L/min on row H. Across about 82 jets that totals roughly **350–400 m³/h**. Pump power follows P = ρgQH/η ([Grundfos](https://www.grundfos.com/in/learn/ecademy/all-courses/the-extended-basic-hydraulic-principles-course-/an-introduction-to-basic-hydraulic-fundamentals)); with nozzle head ≈ 1.25H plus 4 m of losses at η = 0.65, that is about **14 kW of shaft power** (my calculation; recompute with the real row counts). At the economic 1–1.8 m/s pipe velocity, row headers come out at DN80–DN125 and jet branches at DN25–DN50. The suction strainer opening must be smaller than the 10 mm smallest orifice ([Sprayers101](https://sprayers101.com/?p=7438)).

Splash is where the current layout fails. The common rule is that jet height should not exceed the distance to the nearest pool edge ([Delta Fountains](https://www.deltafountains.com/?p=772)), and still-air splash reaches about one jet height ([Safe-Rain](https://saferain.com/en/blog/tips-of-fountain-design-part-2/)). A viewpoint tree puts its tallest jets at the back, which is the worst place for this rule. Row H at 6.4 m needs about 6.4 m behind it but has 1.45 m, and row A at 2.2 m sits 1.4 m from the front edge. The fix is to extend the basin about 5 m rearward, or to add grated wet-deck catch aprons at the back and sides that drain to the basin. Wind control is standard: an anemometer with three programmable stages that lower VFD height, drop the tallest jets, or stop the pumps ([OASE 3 K](https://www.oase.com/en-US/fountain-technology/wind-dependent-fountain-control-3-k-int)). One public fountain shuts down above **6.7 m/s** ([City of Wichita](https://www.wichita.gov/1165/Fountains-at-WaterWalk)). There is a catch specific to this design. Scaling every row's height down together does **not** preserve the alignment from the eye, so the wind stages should recall pre-computed lower "wind-tree" presets, solved from the same perspective formula, rather than a blanket VFD trim. Fog cuts out first, at stage 1.

**Lighting.** The key light is two blade-framed projectors (≥5,000 lm, 30° zoom, green) on masts at X = ±5.5 m, Y ≈ 14 m, lens about 3.4 m high on the canopy-floor sight plane z(Y) = 1.6 + (v_b − 1.6)(14 + Y)/18.5, with the lower blade cut along that plane. The optional fill is 10–11° underwater spots placed behind each jet (azimuth toward the viewer, the reverse of the current script) by about 0.6 × crown height, beam axis at about 55–60° elevation. The trunk gets a 25° base-mounted green spot at least 0.6 m from each foam nozzle. Specify a dark pool floor and a dark backdrop.

**Fog.** A 70 bar pump with 0.3 mm anti-drip nozzles at about 0.11 L/min each: an in-deck ring around the trunk, plus an optional rear fog bar at about 6 m on calm nights only. It runs on a treated, softened supply and is the first stage to cut in wind.

**Pumps.** Eight row VFDs plus one trunk VFD (about 14 kW total), balancing valves per jet.

**Basin.** At least H of clear margin, or catch apron, beyond every jet: about 6.4 m behind row H and at least 2.2 m in front of row A.

**Wind.** Three anemometer stages: fog off, then recall the wind-tree presets, then pumps off. Set the thresholds on site.

## Conclusion

The STAND HERE tree is less a fountain than an anamorphic lighting problem with water as the screen. The columns already exist as soon as the jets run. The canopy is made by deciding which volume the light reaches. The canopy-floor sight plane turns the brief's "floating canopy over faint columns" into one geometric object that a framing blade can cut, and placing the projectors behind and off to the side gets forward-scattered glow without putting a lens in the viewer's eye. That one change does more for the look than any nozzle choice.

The real risk is the unmeasured crown. Every spacing, overlap and parallax margin above scales with crown width, and the only figures available are marketing ranges and an extrapolated break-up correlation. A night test of four nozzle sizes at three heights is the cheapest decision in the project. Its result should be written back into AquaFlow's real-world crown model so that the preview, the pump schedule and the built fountain all use the same measured numbers.
